I just finished reading Cal Newport's [*A World Without Email*](https://www.amazon.com/World-Without-Email-Reimagining-Communication/dp/0525536558). Generally a pretty solid book with some unique insights on how digital technology has made knowledge workers inefficient and miserable in the past two decades (hoping to eventually start posting some formal book reviews on here at some point). Near the end of the book, Cal makes a convincing argument for re-introducing specialization in the modern workplace. The thesis here is that personal computing technology, and email more specifically, has made administrative/general tasks "just easy enough" for highly trained, specialized knowledge workers to do. This has allowed companies to justify reduction of support staff because the trained specialists can now do everything. 

But just because they can, doesn't mean they should. A basic Econ 101 understanding of comparative advantage makes this obvious. But are we ignoring Econ 101 when it comes to AI implementation?

As Cal points out in the book, if the best lawyer in town also happens to be the best tailor, he (or she) should spend all their time on lawyering and outsource tailor activities (lawyering bills at hundreds of dollars per hour). It follows, then, that just because specialized knowledge workers *can* handle more administrative tasks doesn't mean they should - they should devote all their time to the highest-value activities for which they're uniquely suited (lawyering or doctoring or engineering, etc) and outsource support activities (like answering emails, filling out expense reports, submitting travel request, filing for parking contract extensions, etc).

How is comparative advantage talk related to AI?

Most current AI hype is centered around how this tech can effectively replace skilled knowledge work. This could certainly be a future possibility, but the technology is not there yet. It doesn't take much digging on this blog page to realize I'd generally consider myself an AI optimist, but thinking that gen AI can actually replace highly skilled lawyers or engineers or doctors today is delusional. AI *can* be a productivity-boosting tool, [but recent studies show that it has actually increased burnout](https://hbr.org/2026/02/ai-doesnt-reduce-work-it-intensifies-it) and amplified the problems of what Cal calls the *hyperactive hive mind workflow*. In other words, it seems to be actually reducing productivity of knowledge-workers rather than replacing them in a *hyperactive hive-mind workflow* environment.

It's possible that truly maximizing the AI boost within current capability constraints requires a paradigm-shift in how we view the role of these tools in modern knowledge work. Instead of trying to fit a square peg in a round hole, we should start utilizing AI for tasks that it can currently handle autonomously (think digital assistants completing the annoying parking contract renewal applications, handling emails, submitting test requests, tediously debugging code, formatting/typesetting papers, etc). This sort of AI product already exists, but in my experience the performance leaves much to be desired (looking at you, Copilot for Enterprise). Improving effectiveness of digital assistants is infinitely easier than building models capable of replacing senior engineers. More importantly, AI automation of support roles would simultaneously reduce headcount (of support workers) while indirectly boosting productivity of specialized knowledge workers. Freed from the obligation of completing their "just-easy-enough" admin tasks, these knowledge workers could spend more time deeply working without interruption to produce cognitive output that actually moves the needle.

Failing to focus on AI-augmented support roles misses low-hanging fruit that could yield massive productivity gains. While these products already exist, they're not perfect and they certainly don't seem to be the core focus of AI development anymore. Even before AI, we were nowhere near optimizing knowledge work-flows (i.e. we have not yet found knowledge work's equivalent of the assembly line). If there are still massive productivity gains to be had by improving workflow efficiency, why do we think we need to replace knowledge workers with AI ASAP? While I admire the big swing-for-the-fences mentality regarding AI's role in knowledge work, starting smaller and iterating quickly might be more effective.
