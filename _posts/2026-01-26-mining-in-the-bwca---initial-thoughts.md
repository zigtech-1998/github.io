[A bill recently passed the Minnesota house](https://www.savetheboundarywaters.org/bill-passed-house-overturn-mining-ban-near-boundary-waters) to lift the mining ban protecting the Boundary Waters watershed from copper mining. As an annual BWCA enjoyer, this topic hits pretty close to home. I've never considered myself an environmental activist, but having some actual skin in the game on this particular issue has caused me to pay a little more attention to this particular issue than for similar issues in the past. A BWCA trip is an annual tradition for a group of close childhood friends, and this wilderness area is truly a unique and special place. I've heard there are, at most, a [dozen places left in the U.S. that are considered "truly free" areas](https://www.theinertia.com/environment/there-are-only-12-quiet-places-left-in-the-u-s-and-this-man-will-only-tell-us-3-of-them/) (free of man-made light and noise pollution), and the BWCA is one of them. Having experienced it first-hand, its remote and isolated beauty are certainly worth protecting.

That said, as a subscriber to the [e/acc philosophy](https://beff.substack.com/p/notes-on-eacc-principles-and-tenets), I understand that there are trade-offs here. The demand for metals like copper and nickel will only accelerate with the rise of AI, electric vehicles, and other emerging (and critically important) technologies. The ability to produce these resources domestically will be critical to sustained U.S. economic and technological leadership on the world stage. Projects like these require a nuanced evaluation of risks and returns. I've spent a decent bit of mental energy weighing these trade-offs for similar projects like the (in)famous [Trans-Alaskan Pipeline system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Alaska_Pipeline_System), but never dedicated any significant time to actual research on the subject. Seeing as I have a personal stake in this one, I think it's worth spending a deeper dive into the pros and cons of the Twin Mines project specifically, and domestic mining more broadly.

**Benefits:**
A necessary part of an accurate risk-benefit analysis of this issue is contextualizing the risks with the mine's projected productive output. By the numbers, the Twin Mines project should produce:
- jobs
- net economic revenue/benefit
- overall U.S. demand for copper and nickel
- cons of outsourcing - net environmental loss by outsourcing mining operations to countries with poor or non-existant environmental controls

Importantly, a proper risk-benefit analysis also needs to consider the risks of *inaction*. In other words, if we don't build this mine (or any others like it), how will the U.S. copper/nickel demand be satisfied? What happens if we fail to meet the domestic demand for these metals?

**Risks:**
- risk of severe and permanent damage to the watershed
- impact on wilderness quality even absent a heavy metal leak/disastrous accident
	- will the BWCA still be a "Truly Free" place if we decide to mine in the watershed? Even if the mining operation was guaranteed accident-free?
- Are there alternative ways of satisfying copper and nickel demand domestically?
	- Arizona mine options that aren't near a "Truly Free" area?
	-  Innovative ways to [satisfy AI demand with orders of magnitude less energy input](https://extropic.ai/) (and, by extension, maybe less raw material input?)
	- Can we on-shore copper recycling operations?

**Twin Mines Verdict:**
As of the writing of this intro/skeleton I haven't done much research. I especially don't know why we need nickel so bad, what the alternative means of domestic nickel production are, etc. However, if I had to make a decision today, I would vote agains the Twin Mines establishment. The reasons for this are:
- my personal ties to the BWCA
- the potential for alternatives in satisfying domestic copper demand
- risk of massive permanent damage
- risk of wilderness disruption absent a heavy metal leak or accident
