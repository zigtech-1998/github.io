AI is a good tool for doing many things faster, but it doesn’t address the key bottleneck in knowledge work and therefore can neither replace humans nor completely revolutionize knowledge work in its current state. Chamath is saying as much in this [recent tweet:](https://x.com/chamath/status/2075845619608891882?s=46)

>In other words, we find the last 5% (ie where a model is truly equivalent to a reasonable engineer) extremely difficult to achieve and extremely expensive to such a degree that the fully loaded cost of the model + the engineer will not pay for itself

>I wonder how many engineers, in all orgs, are running amok right now by using the latest frontier models as a kind of slot machine. Increasingly turning their mind off, largely keeping productivity flat while their CEO and CFO deals with a massive token bill?

In other words, the "last 5%" that Chamath is talking about requires focused work from real engineers with deep knowledge and years of experience on the specific subject/task. What AI is currently giving us is a highly sophisticated, efficient tool for automating tasks & generating text. Real people still need to polish the automation frameworks and develop a cohesive understanding about what the generated text/code should do. 

This is exactly what Cal Newport was writing about in [his recent blog](https://calnewport.com/on-bottlenecks-and-productivity/) post on productivity bottlenecks:

>Something I’ve long written about is the reality that many digital productivity tools paradoxically make us _busier_, rather than _better_ at our jobs.... When we deploy a digital tool like email to speed up communication, or generative AI to create (sloppy) slide presentations quickly, we don’t automatically become better at our jobs. If these steps don’t improve the bottleneck in our process – the key link where the real value is produced – then.... they’re just as likely to create pile-ups and distraction, without actually boosting our true productivity.

Both excerpts describe why many predictions of AI's impact on jobs & industry have fallen flat. The key bottleneck still lies with real people doing focused, difficult cognitive work. This is still the main value producer in the knowledge work economy, and no amount of pressure from upper management to "drive AI adoption" is going to change this fact.

You might be wondering where all my AI optimism went? Well, I'm still an AI optimist in the sense that I think it is a great tool for some things (code generation, web development, etc) which can give early-stage entrepreneurs and start-ups more leverage to create custom solutions without having to learn code or web dev from scratch. I also think it is a phenomenal tool for didactic learning. However, both these examples still require a highly motivated & curious human being to drive value production. In the case of larger enterprises, I think the value of AI is much more limited by bureaucratic red tape preventing would-be innovators from implementing change enabled by the combination of their human initiative & AI's generative capabilities.

Unless something changes massively in the next 3-5 years (which is certainly possible), it's looking more and more likely that AI stick around for the long term as a great knowledge work tool, but will not spawn New World Order in knowledge work nor replace entire sectors of the of the white collar economy.
