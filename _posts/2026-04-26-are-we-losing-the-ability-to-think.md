From Tyler Cowen's excellent [*Goodbye to the Age of the Book* ](https://www.thefp.com/p/goodbye-to-the-age-of-the-book )article for [**The Free Press**.](https://www.thefp.com/):

> The Western world is continuing to move away from a very special enlightenment period when book reading and literary modes of thinking were paramount.

These literary modes of thinking were not just paramount in importance, but were pervasive across all classes of society. Regardless of profession or higher education status, everybody [used to read serious books and carry thoughtful opinions on culture and politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Intellectual_Life_of_the_British_Working_Classes), which would be openly discussed & debated. It feels important to over-emphasize that this habit was not reserved for the intellectuals, knowledge-workers, or the bourgeoisie. Laborers and working-class families were highly self-educated simply from spending leisure time reading books. 

Of course, these "literary modes" of deep thinking were essential to our incredible scientific and technological achievements of the 20th century. When NASA landed on the moon in 1969, engineers & astronauts communicated in long-form memos (I think [Bezos is mandating something similar](https://www.cnbc.com/2019/10/14/jeff-bezos-this-is-the-smartest-thing-we-ever-did-at-amazon.html) at his companies). We accomplished countless other scientific, cultural, and political advances by using human-generated, long-form text as a primary method of knowledge generation, communication, and storage. It facilitated deep, focused work and thoughtful critique & refinement of ideas. In Tyler's words,

> A statement or argument exists in fixed, immutable form and can be evaluated as such. Peer review and commentary are eased and enabled. It is no accident that most science is presented in the form of text rather than video. It might later be taught through video, but the ideas are best evaluated through writing, both on the presentation side and the criticism side.
> ....
> Oral[^1] culture, in contrast, tends to be more fluid, harder to evaluate and verify, more prone to rumor, and it has fewer gatekeepers.

With the resurgence of "oral" culture, people just want a bulleted slide deck, a three-sentence executive summary, or pictures. It seems that even engineers and technical-types refuse to read any detail anymore. Rather than expending mental effort to interpret the necessary detail, the audience blames the presenter for "poor presentation skills" if *any* long-form analysis is presented. Like Tyler said, there is a reason new, important, complicated science is presented in long form text rather than pictures and bullets.

In my view, this aversion to long-form writing is driven by the attention economy and the hyperactive hive mind culture in modern knowledge work. Outside the office, we are constantly bombarded with infinite demands on our attention which are intentionally engineered to provide maximum intrigue for minimal effort. At work, we are told by out-of-touch managers to "use more AI" to get things done faster while being constantly distracted by an infinitely growing stream of IMs and emails. This strategy may feel more productive (busy) in the moment, but it's making us all miserable and is ruining our ability to focus deeply on any complex task, which ultimately is not optimal for problem solving.

Is this apocalyptic? Probably not. Society has generally found ways to evolve along with disruptive technological advances. Is this move (reversion?) towards "oral" culture a good thing? Also no. I see merit in both Tyler's optimistic & pessimistic takes on how the declining importance of long-form text will affect our future:

> The optimistic interpretation of our situation is that reading longer works has been in decline for a long time, and overall our civilization has managed the transition fairly well. Across history we have had various balances of written and oral cultures, and if some further rebalancing is required in the direction of the oral, we should be able to make that work, just as we have done in the past. The rise of television, whatever you may think of it, did not do us in.

>A second and more pessimistic diagnosis is that print and reading culture has been hanging by a thread, and current and pending technological advances are about to give that thread its final cut. The intellectual and cultural apocalypse is near. Even if your family thinks of itself as well-educated, your kids will grow up unable to work their way through a classic novel. They will watch the _Lord of the Rings_ movies, but never pick up the books. As a result, they are likely to have less scientific and analytic objectivity, and they will embody some of the worst and most volatile aspects of TikTok culture. They will, however, be able to sample large numbers of small bits of information, or sometimes misinformation, in a short period of time.

**"The intellectual and cultural apocalypse is near. Even if your family thinks of itself as well-educated, your kids will grow up unable to work their way through a classic novel**".... yuck. Not a good sign, even if only partially true. If we lose our ability to engage in deep, focused thought, we'll merely be directors and prompt generators for the production of AI slop. How can we expect our children's generation to be more happy or productive at work if they're spamming AI chatbots with prompts rather than actually producing something valuable themselves? Our best safeguard against this depressing future is to retain our ability to read long books, write in a coherent, structured format, and to focus on challenging problems form more than 5 minutes at a time.
