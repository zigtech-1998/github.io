Marginal Revolution never fails to produce good content. About a month ago, TC posted [a few bullets on the AI impact to GDP](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/05/ai-in-gdp.html), summarizing a couple interesting points from a new [policy brief ](https://www.piie.com/publications/policy-briefs/2026/where-ai-gdp-statistics)from [Peterson Institute for International Economics](https://www.piie.com/). 

The companion paper cited in the policy brief measures AI output as "the generation of AI compute." The figure below is taken from the Methods section of the paper:

![2026 05 28 Fig 1  AI Production Outline, Korinek et al, 2026](/github.io/assets/images/ai-gdp-and-goodharts-law-2026-05-28-fig-1--ai-production-outline-korinek-et-al-2026.png)

This model captures the flow of AI CapEx (infrastructure and model investment) and OpEx (electrical power) through to AI inference output. Like any product unconstrained by regulators in a free market economy, investment by profit-driven entrepreneurs is increasing the quality of the product while simultaneously decreasing the price. The policy brief claims that "AI compute spending grew by more than 140 percent per year each in 2024 and 2025, raw compute capacity by more than 200 percent per year, and quality-adjusted AI output by more than 2,000 percent per year." This claim is supported by **Figure 2** in the companion paper:
![2026 05 28 Fig 2 - Price of inference tokens](/github.io/assets/images/ai-gdp-and-goodharts-law-2026-05-28-fig-2--price-of-inference-tokens.png)

Ok, great - we've established that AI is yet another example of a free enterprise system increasing quality and decreasing prices of valuable products. It is growing rapidly, with significant capital influx and an apparent increase in quality output per unit spend. Despite this data from the paper itself, the policy brief opens with a shocking statement:

> "The artificial intelligence (AI) economy in the United States is growing at extraordinary rates of over 2,000 percent per year yet is leaving only a small mark in the nation’s GDP figures. This is a measurement gap that, left unaddressed, will become a policy gap—because what cannot be measured cannot be steered."

If it's improving so rapidly, why would we want to steer it? Are we trying to kill it? While I don't think AI has fully lived up to all of its hype, this type of rapidly advancing technology is inherently unpredictable. This means two things: (1) trying to intelligently steer it with central planning is a fool's errand, and (2) killing AI progress through regulation will significantly limit the American economy's potential. While it may not live up to all the hype today, who knows what its capabilities will be 5 years from now if we just keep the bureaucrats' hands out of it and let the entrepreneurs cook?

Throughput efficiency gains and economic policy aside, what this dataset can't tell us is whether the AI output is actually accomplishing its mission. This model does capture how the AI industry itself is growing. It does not show any evidence that AI is measurably increasing knowledge work output, let alone actually replacing human knowledge workers. While the quality is improving and the cost of tokens is decreasing, AI companies are still not massively profitable. Their models are also not causing wholesale layoffs in any particular industry. While it's certainly an effective tool for productivity gains in certain areas (see [Cal Newport's predictions on AI and Knowledge Work](https://calnewport.com/ai)), it has not yet fully lived up to all the hype. 

This paper is making the common mistake of conflating *activity* with *productivity* (a la Cal Newport). It is measuring the *activity* of the AI sector, but is not answering the much harder question of determining the impact of AI's output on actual knowledge work productivity. While it may be true that we have a measurement gap in measuring the AI industry specifically, the data presented do not necessarily support the claim that AI is driving actual productivity gains for its users. If we are optimizing for capital investment or token efficiency maxxing, these data show AI is improving rapidly. But are these really the right metrics? It feels like this is a textbook case of [Goodhart's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), similar to paying developers for lines of code written or total pull requests rather than linking compensation to the actual utility or value creation of the end product. If you look at knowledge work productivity improvements downstream of AI rather than looking at model production/output as an end in itself, is the picture still as rosy as the new [policy brief ](https://www.piie.com/publications/policy-briefs/2026/where-ai-gdp-statistics) implies?
