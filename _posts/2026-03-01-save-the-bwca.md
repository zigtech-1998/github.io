[A bill recently passed the Minnesota house](https://www.savetheboundarywaters.org/bill-passed-house-overturn-mining-ban-near-boundary-waters) to lift the mining ban protecting the Boundary Waters watershed from copper mining. As an annual BWCA enjoyer, this topic hits pretty close to home. I've never considered myself an environmental activist, but having some actual skin in the game on this particular issue has caused me to pay a little more attention to this particular issue than for similar issues in the past. A BWCA trip is an annual tradition for a group of close childhood friends, and this wilderness area is truly a unique and special place. Pictures will never do it justice, but still do more lifting than words when trying to convey how special this place is to the uninitiated:

I've heard there are, at most, a [dozen places left in the U.S. that are considered "truly free" areas](https://www.theinertia.com/environment/there-are-only-12-quiet-places-left-in-the-u-s-and-this-man-will-only-tell-us-3-of-them/) (free of man-made light and noise pollution), and the BWCA is one of them. Having experienced it first-hand, its remote and isolated beauty are certainly worth protecting.

That said, as a subscriber to the [e/acc philosophy](https://beff.substack.com/p/notes-on-eacc-principles-and-tenets), I understand that there are trade-offs here. The demand for metals like copper and nickel will only accelerate with the rise of AI, electric vehicles, and other emerging (and critically important) technologies. Domestic production of these resources will be critical to sustained U.S. economic and technological leadership on the world stage. Projects like these require a nuanced evaluation of risks and returns. I've spent a decent bit of mental energy weighing these trade-offs for similar projects like the (in)famous [Trans-Alaskan Pipeline system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Alaska_Pipeline_System), but never dedicated significant time to actual research on the subject. Seeing as I have a personal stake in this one, I think it's worth a deeper dive into the pros and cons of the Twin Metals project specifically.

*Risks of Inaction*
Importantly, a proper risk-benefit analysis also needs to consider the risks of *inaction*. In other words, if we don't build this mine (or any others like it), how will the U.S. copper/nickel demand be satisfied? Failing to meet this demand is not an option. A discussion of why we cannot afford a shortage of these essential metals is a topic for a different essay (or maybe even a few books). So, assuming that a shortage of these metals is not a viable option, we will have to import more copper and nickel from outside the U.S. to meet this demand. In other words, we will simply be exporting the environmental and labor risk (and economic benefits) elsewhere. When using Claude as a research aid for this topic, the LLM put it very bluntly: 

> "**The brutal calculus:** A decision to block domestic mining in Minnesota to protect the Boundary Waters potentially exports not just environmental impact, but human mortality risk to populations with far less political power and far fewer alternatives"

Yikes.

But is it really that bad, or is Claude just being dramatic? Thankfully, U.S. gets much of it's copper and nickel imports from [Canada](https://elements.visualcapitalist.com/americas-import-reliance-of-critical-minerals-charted/) and [Chile](https://think.ing.com/articles/industrial-metals-monthly-us-copper-tariff-risks-pull-more-metal-to-the-us/), which have metal ore mining safety profiles comparable to the U.S. However, Peru and the Congo, which provide a minority share of U.S. copper imports, have significantly more lethal mining operations. Increasing import demand for these minerals by blocking Twin Metals could increase exposure in these riskier countries. Additionally, there is a price-dependent fatality risk in even relatively safe countries like Chile. [Arratia-Solar, et. al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301420723001976), reports:

> *We find that the relationship between the international copper price and the incidence of mining fatalities in the country is best explained by a quadratic function, which shows that once copper value surpassed the threshold of $5200 per tonne, the association is markedly positive –the higher the value, the more fatalities are reported in the country.*

This trend *could* hold true in the U.S. as well, but I suspect our far more risk-averse regulatory environment and headline-seeking media outlets would make sure there are huge consequences if this trend holds on U.S. soil.

On the nickel side, Indonesia is projected to become a dominant global producer of refined nickel. While export bans prevent the U.S. from sourcing nickel ore from Indonesia, it is possible that blocking the Twin Lakes mine could increase domestic demand for Indonesian refined nickel. While nickel mining and smelting safety in Indonesia is unknown due to lack of data, the lack of data in itself is not encouraging. Indonesia's nickel industry was propped up by Chinese support (a country with abysmal mining safety history), and safety/regulatory oversight in Indonesia's smelting operations is being actively blocked. According to [The Interpreter](https://www.lowyinstitute.org/the-interpreter/glimpse-indonesia-s-nickel-policy):

> *Our visit to the smelter was restricted, forcing us to observe from outside the gate. Local government officials face similar obstacles in obtaining permits to inspect and monitor the site. Such barriers underscore a troubling trend of [secrecy and impunity](https://www.scmp.com/week-asia/economics/article/3247493/indonesias-chinese-funded-nickel-smelters-lives-put-stake-safety-fears-mount) across Indonesia’s nickel sector, raising serious concerns about accountability and transparency.
> 
> The smelter’s location in a residential area has exposed residents to harmful industrial emissions. The rapidly expanding settlement nearby suffers from poor planning and inadequate public input. Locals say it resembles a slum rather than a new industrial town.*
 
Obviously, mining in the U.S. is not without risk either. I alluded to the danger of the Twin Lakes jobs earlier. In the U.S., metal ore mining had a [~5x higher fatality rate than overall private industry in 2021](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2023/mining-fatalities-rose-21-8-percent-from-2020-to-2021.htm). That said, the modern world would cease to exist without these jobs. Like it or not, somebody has to do it. And, in terms of absolute risk, U.S. mining jobs are still pretty safe ([.0196% fatality rate in metal ore mining vs 0.0038% in overall private industry](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2023/mining-fatalities-rose-21-8-percent-from-2020-to-2021.htm)). The same cannot be said for copper and nickel exporters like Peru, Congo, and Indonesia. While the magnitude of this risk is unclear given that we mostly import copper & nickel from developed countries, the environmental and human impact of outsourcing this work is often conspicuously absent from discussions about domestic mining operations but certainly merits thoughtful consideration.

So, it seems that there is a real (if not small) labor safety advantage to mining domestically vs. outsourcing abroad. That said, all of this hinges on the assumption that the Twin Metals output will provide a significant portion of the U.S. Copper and nickel demand. In other words, to make this relative risk reduction worth it, there has to be a significant unmet need for domestic copper/nickel ore mining. If not, the risk/benefit calculus changes dramatically. Why should our sacred Truly Free environments be put at risk to satisfy metal demand abroad? 

*Raw Material Supply*
According to a [pre-feasibility study from 2014](https://www.mining-technology.com/projects/twin-metals-minnesota-tmm-mine-minnesota/?cf-view) shows that the mine *"anticipates production of 5.8 billion pounds (Blb) of copper, 1.2Blbs of nickel, 1.5 million ounces (Moz) of platinum, 4Moz of palladium, 1Moz of gold and 25.2Moz of silver over the initial 30-year operational life."* Studying the accuracy of these prediction studies is outside the scope of this article, so let's assume these numbers are reasonably accurate. Those are big numbers... seems like an incredibly productive and impactful project, right? Well, maybe not as much as one might think... [*Save the Boundary Waters*](https://www.savetheboundarywaters.org/release-new-report-details-how-twin-metals-greenwashing-risky-mining-next-boundary-waters) project cites indicates that this represents only a tiny fraction of the total US domestic demand:

>*"Based on 2019 figures from the U.S. Geological Survey and on Twin Metals' own production projections, a Twin Metals mine would provide amounts equal to only 2.3% of copper consumption, 3.6% of nickel consumption, and 1.5% of cobalt consumption in the U.S. annually."*

Further, much of this ore is likely to be shipped overseas for low-cost smelting (likely in China). Interestingly, the mine would not be owned by American enterprises. Twin metals is a subsidiary of [Antofagasta Minerals](https://www.antofagasta.co.uk/), which is controlled by a Chilean conglomerate. In other words, they have no incentive to sell the mine's output in U.S. markets. This mine's output seems like small peanuts at best, even if the output was exclusively sold to US customers. The reality is that this Chilean-run sulfide mine is likely to sell these minerals abroad, while the BWCA bears the risk.

*[[Jobs and Economic Growth]]*
So what benefit would the U.S. derive from this operation? Surely something this large should provide a huge local economic boom by brining huge jobs and infrastructure to the region, right? Well, the answer is complicated. First we should look at how many jobs this project will actually create. I am generally against job creation as the core driver for green-lighting a project. Congresspeople lobby for job creation projects because it wins them re-election, but it also incentivizes inefficiencies & bloat.[^1] However, the Twin Metals project is not run by the government and is not solely designed as a jobs program. The mine will yield tangible economic output - jobs are just necessary side effect (benefit). Additionally, the mine is likely to operate for the better part of the next 3 decades. This time horizon is long enough to provide entire careers with above-average wages. Such jobs include mining machine operators, underground operators, supervisors, engineers, environmental, health, and safety (EH&S) specialists, mechanics, transport/logistics workers, and all the ancillary local economic benefit that comes with adding [750+ 6-figure salaries](https://www.twin-metals.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/TMM_FactSheet_December2019.pdf) to a small midwestern town. Importantly, these higher-paying jobs are mostly held by folks without a college degree, offering significant upward mobility for those without access to a college education. Going one layer deeper, these high paying jobs are an opportunity for local blue-collar laborers to turn a school education into brighter future for their kids. This work comes with risks, but the monetary payoff is significant, especially for people who did not have the privilege of higher education or white collar work. Ironically, many of the folks who would block the Twin Metals project are from the more privileged and educated classes. Of course, the mine owners and capitalists would be getting filthy rich off this project. On the other hand, one could spin this storyline as the privileged and educated bourgeoise blocking opportunity for proletariate's upward mobility, thereby contributing to the growing wealth gap in this country.

Ok, so we've established that this project will create a lot of jobs and infrastructure in the region. This must be better than the alternative, right? According to [Harvard economists,](https://stock.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/analysis-proposed-20-year-mineral-leasing-withdrawal-superior-national-forest) maintaining the outdoors/wilderness economic model and restricting copper sulfide mining would likely result in better economic output over the potential life of the mine. It's important to note that this analysis does not include what would happen *after* the mine would close in 30+ years. Environmental effects are likely to last long after the mine closes, which would hurt the environmental economy after the mine ceases operations. From [the paper, titled *Analysis of Proposed 20-year Mineral Leasing Withdrawal in Superior National Forest*:](https://stock.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/analysis-proposed-20-year-mineral-leasing-withdrawal-superior-national-forest)

>*"The environmental risks associated with sulfide-ore copper mining within the watershed of the BWCAW are potentially economically consequential.... In a study of acid mine drainage-impaired lakes in rural Ohio, Mishra et al. (2012) find a negative relationship between sulfate levels in impaired lakes and recreational use. The literature (reviewed below) documenting the transition of amenity-rich communities from reliance on extractive industries to tourism-based growth suggests a link between the two: were sulfide-ore copper mining to proceed at the TMM site, a contraction in tourism and recreation-based economic activity could plausibly occur, depending on the extent of mining disamenities that diminish the wilderness experience as well as on the severity of spills, breaches, and/or drainage."*

**Final Verdict**
I won't spend too much time on a in-depth analysis of the risks here. It's not a stretch to state that the presence of a massive sulfide mine in the BWCAW watershed area presents a risk of severe and permanent damage to the watershed. As stated in the [Harvard economics paper](https://stock.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5911/files/stock/files/1-s2.0-s0921800919309954-main.pdf), even an accident-free mining operation will disrupt the tourism economy and destroy much of the BWCAW's unique beauty as a Truly Free place.

Since this domestic operation will be risking one of the most unique and beautiful natural land reserves in the country, the payoff needs to be extremely high. When looking directly at the potential benefits of relative mining risk reduction, job creation, and raw material supply, the benefits appear very small to the U.S. economy. **On all three counts, this project falls well short of producing outsized economic benefit relative to the natural resource risks**. In this case, due diligence and a bit of honest research led me to the same conclusion as my tree-hugging friends who reflexively cringe and any economic project to harness our natural resources. Turns our leasing one of our most sacred Truly Free areas to a Chilean mining conglomerate to provide relatively tiny amounts of copper and nickel to the world market isn't a great trade-off.

To make it crystal clear: **we should absolutely do everything in our power, as Minnesotans and as Americans, to prevent Twin Metals from sulfate mining in the BWCA.**

If you feel as strongly about this issue as I do, please consider visiting https://www.savetheboundarywaters.org/ to find out how you can help protect the BWCA.
