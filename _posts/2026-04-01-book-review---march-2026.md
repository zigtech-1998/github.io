## *Skunk Works*
**Author: Ben Rich**
A great first-hand account of high stakes, cutting edge engineering from one of the giants in the U.S. Aerospace tradition. Great case study on how a small, agile team of experts can create world-breaking technology shockingly fast. Also a great case study on how government/bureaucratic interference skyrockets costs and busts schedules. *Not* a case study on how this interference affects product quality (the Skunk Works always produced awesome shit, regardless of the roadblocks), but shares themes with [Johnny Boyd's complaints about the military industrial complex](https://www.amazon.com/Boyd-Fighter-Pilot-Who-Changed/dp/0316796883).

From an engineering perspective, its truly amazing what Kelly Johnson, Ben Rich, and co. were able to accomplish. Even today, their feats of engineering on the SR-71 and the F-117A are awe-inspiring. Hell, the U-2, which they built during the cold war, is still flying *today.* Studying this book, and studying the Skunk Works generally, can help us revive the Space-Race era pace of innovation. Really looking forward to finishing Kelly Johnson's autobiography in April.

## *A World Without Email* - Cal Newport
**Author: Cal Newport**
A solid personal productivity book. As always, Newport's writing style is very accessible, engaging, and easy to follow. The book develops the thesis that email (or what he more broadly refers to as the "Hyperactive Hive Mind workflow") is making us less productive and miserable. This thesis is followed by proposals for systematically minimizing drawbacks of digital communication while still realizing the benefits these tools can provide.

Overall I think his thesis is largely correct. I also think the ideas for combating/mitigating this problem are well researched and supported by real-world examples. Unfortunately, most of this practical advice centers on broad, organization-level systems. In other words, implementing many of these strategies requires influence over group-level or business-level policies. They're not simple individual productivity systems that can be seamlessly implemented by individual contributors. Unfortunately, simple individual productivity systems cannot tame the hyperactive hive mind beast on their own (Cal even explicitly states that *"...batching your inbox checks - a commonly suggested "solution" to improving your experience with email - is not necessarily a panacea*"). For individual contributors or young, early career professionals, the reality is we must find a way to collaborate in a system where hyperactive hive mind workflow is the entrenched incumbent. 

That said, there are still some good nuggets that are transferrable to the individual level. I'm currently experimenting with some of these myself. Overall I think its a step in the right direction, but I'm still in the rough initial period where I feel like I'm spending too much time implementing & planning within the new system rather than actually executing. This is particularly true when it comes to managing multiple project boards (the book really hypes up project boards as a workflow management system). This imbalance is a real red flag, and if it continues I'll definitely need to lean the system out. 

TL;DR: The book is certainly worth the read, but I'd caution the reader that a lot of the advice could be (a) not applicable to you as an individual contributor, or (b) a little tricky to implement on a individual scale without bloat.

## *Project Hail Mary*
**Author: Andy Weir**
Brought back the fun in reading. Lost a lot of sleep over the two-ish weeks I spent reading this title. Reminded me of how I'd get lost in fiction books as a kid, before cell phones and the internet and adult responsibilities were constantly stealing my attention. A pure joy to read, with engaging, well thought out sci-fi. Weir masterfully splits each chapter's narrative between the present events and flashbacks, keeping the reader on the edge of their seat with just enough historical context while maintaining an exciting level of mystery. There's a good reason Weir's books have produced two major motion pictures (the whimsical, upbeat tone in the face of deadly stakes immediately reminded me of the Martian). 

Speaking of - the movie was solid, too. I've always found that I get a lot more enjoyment out of movies if I've read the book first. This is especially true when long, dense books are adapted to the screen. Every detail from the novel's nearly 500 pages obviously could not fit into a single movie. Having read the book helped me catch some small easter-egg type references that viewers unfamiliar with the book would have missed (I found the same to be true for American Prometheus / Oppenheimer).
